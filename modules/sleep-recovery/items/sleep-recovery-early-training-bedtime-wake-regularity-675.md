refraction_notes:
  - note: >
      THE WEEKEND LIE-IN MOVES MONDAY'S CLOCK. Sleeping in about three hours
      on a weekend delayed the clock by about 32 minutes by Monday and left
      people sleepier on Monday and Tuesday (16 young adults). Two weeks of
      waking three hours later delayed melatonin onset by 2.4 hours (14
      adults). For someone who wakes at 04:30 on training days, an 08:00
      weekend wake is a 3.5-hour shift, so Sunday's 21:00 lights-out lands
      earlier on the body clock than on the wall clock: lying awake that
      night is the expected result, not a sleep problem. The direction is
      well supported; the minutes in a real lifter's week are not measured.
    grade: B
  - note: >
      THE SLEEP NEED IS PERSONAL, THE FLOOR IS NOT A TARGET. 7 hours is a
      population floor (013) and the athlete consensus recommends an
      individual approach that weighs the athlete's own perceived need
      rather than one number. For a 04:30 wake that still means lights-out
      near 21:00 for most people (071). Someone who reliably sleeps less
      without daytime sleepiness need not be pushed to 7 h; someone who
      needs more cannot get it from a 21:00 lights-out and 04:30 alarm.
    grade: D
  - note: >
      FEELING FINE IS NOT THE TEST. Under 6 h a night for two weeks,
      deficits kept growing while people rated themselves only a little
      sleepy -- they were largely unaware of it. A 05:00 lifter on a run of
      short nights who says they feel fine is the expected pattern; the
      nightly record is the better witness. Endpoints were attention and
      cognition, not strength.
    grade: B
  - note: >
      EVENING ROOM LIGHT IS A REAL LEVER; GLASSES ARE NOT. Ordinary room
      light in the hours before bed delayed melatonin onset in almost
      everyone tested and shortened it by about 90 minutes, against dim
      light. The 2022 expert consensus puts numbers on it: dim from at least
      3 hours before bedtime, dark for sleep, bright by day. For a 21:00
      lights-out that is lighting lowered from about 18:00, which in
      practice means lower, warmer lamps after dinner and a darker bedroom,
      not orange glasses (012) and not a lux meter. The thresholds are a
      consensus, not a tested dose for this schedule.
    grade: B
  - note: >
      DAYLIGHT IS THE ANCHOR, AND THE CLOCK FOLLOWS WAKE TIME. A week of
      natural light only moved the clock about 2 hours earlier, most in
      later chronotypes, and a weekend of camping undid the post-weekend
      delay. Whether 04:30 gym light helps or hurts depends on where it
      falls against the body-temperature low (travel 002), which a lifter
      cannot know; daylight outdoors after the session is the safe half of
      the advice. Both camping studies are small field studies.
    grade: C
  - note: >
      A WARM SHOWER OR BATH 1-2 HOURS BEFORE BED HELPS A LITTLE. Pooled small
      studies of 40-42.5 C water for as little as 10 minutes, 1-2 hours
      before bed, shortened the time to fall asleep and improved self-rated
      sleep. That fits a lifter whose dinner, shower and wind-down share the
      hours before an early lights-out. It is a nudge of minutes; as a
      standalone treatment for insomnia, sleep hygiene is advised against
      (007), and persistent trouble falling asleep goes to CBT-I.
    grade: C
claim: >
  For someone lifting at 05:00-06:00 the night before is where the session is
  won or lost. The clock follows wake time: a weekend lie-in of about 3 hours
  delayed the clock about 32 minutes by Monday and raised Monday and Tuesday
  sleepiness, and two weeks of 3 h later waking delayed melatonin onset 2.4
  hours -- so after a late weekend, the early lights-out on the eve of the
  first training day comes before the body is ready, and lying awake then is
  expected. Ordinary room light before bed delays melatonin onset in almost
  everyone, and an expert consensus asks for dim light from about 3 hours
  before bed, darkness for sleep and bright light by day; natural daylight
  moves the clock earlier. A warm shower or bath 1-2 hours before bed
  shortens sleep onset by minutes. Sleep need is individual around a 7-hour
  population floor, and under chronic restriction deficits keep growing while
  people feel only slightly sleepy, so the nightly record, not how the lifter
  feels, shows whether the schedule works. No study tested any of this in
  people training at 05:00-06:00; every clock time is a transfer.
reasoning: >
  The row exists because 071 gives the early lifter a bedtime anchor but says
  nothing about why that bedtime fails after a weekend, what the evening
  should look like, or how to read "I feel fine". The two wake-time studies
  are within-person and controlled and point the same way, which carries the
  direction at B; their samples (14 and 16 young adults) do not carry a
  minute count for a free-living lifter. Gooley 2011 is large for a lab
  study and the result is near-universal, so the evening-light direction is
  B, while Brown 2022's thresholds are an expert consensus over that lab
  base and are spoken as such. Social jetlag as an exposure, its BMI
  association and the contested status of weekend catch-up already sit in
  014 and are linked, not repeated. Van Dongen 2003 is the cleanest evidence
  that subjective sleepiness under-reads chronic restriction, which is why
  the brief leans on sleep_min patterns rather than self-report for the
  schedule question. The warm-water meta-analysis and the camping studies are
  small and carried at C. Walsh 2021's individual-need line is consensus (D)
  and its toolbox was not read. Not contested: no located source argues the
  opposite direction for any statement here; the open questions are size and
  transfer.

## What the evidence says

| Statement | Source | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Weekend sleep-in about 3 h -> clock about 32 min later Monday, sleepier Mon/Tue | Taylor 2008, n=16 | controlled, small |
| Waking 3 h later for 2 weeks -> melatonin onset 2.4 h later | Burgess & Eastman 2005, n=14 | controlled, small |
| Room light before bed -> later melatonin onset in 99 percent, about 90 min shorter | Gooley 2011, n=116 | controlled lab |
| Evening at most 10 lx melanopic from 3 h before bed; sleep at most 1 lx; day at least 250 lx | Brown 2022 | expert consensus |
| Natural light only -> clock about 2 h earlier; weekend camping undoes the weekend delay | Wright 2013, Stothard 2017 | small field studies |
| Warm water 40-42.5 C, 1-2 h before bed, 10 min+ -> faster sleep onset | Haghayegh 2019, 17 studies | meta, small studies |
| Chronic 6 h nights: deficits grow, sleepiness ratings lag | Van Dongen 2003 | controlled dose-response |
| Sleep need is individual; weigh perceived need | Walsh 2021 | consensus |

Not here, linked instead: social jetlag and BMI, weekend catch-up (014); the
7.5 h anchor and the 05:00 slot (071); blue-light glasses (012); sleep hygiene
vs CBT-I (007).

## 한국어 요약 (답변용)

- 새벽 4시 30분에 일어나면 7시간을 자려고 9시 전후에 불을 끈다(071). 7시간은 인구 기준의 최저선이지 모두의
  목표는 아니다. 필요한 잠은 사람마다 다르다.
- 주말에 3시간쯤 늦잠을 자면 월요일 아침까지 몸 시계가 약 30분 늦춰지고 월·화에 더 졸렸다(16명). 3시간 늦게
  일어나는 생활을 2주 하면 멜라토닌 시작이 2.4시간 늦어졌다(14명).
- 그래서 주말에 늦게 일어난 뒤 일요일 밤 9시에 누우면 잠이 안 오는 게 정상이다. 수면 문제로 보지 않는다.
- 잠들기 전 몇 시간 동안의 일반 실내조명도 멜라토닌 시작을 늦췄다(거의 모든 사람에서). 전문가 합의는 잠자기
  3시간 전부터 조명을 어둡게, 잘 때는 캄캄하게, 낮에는 밝게를 권한다. 9시 취침이면 저녁 먹은 뒤 조명을 낮추고
  따뜻한 색으로. 블루라이트 안경은 효과가 확인되지 않았다(012).
- 낮에 바깥 햇빛을 받으면 몸 시계가 앞당겨진다. 운동 끝나고 밖에서 햇빛을 받는 것이 안전한 쪽이다.
- 자기 1~2시간 전 따뜻한 샤워·목욕(40~42.5도, 10분 이상)은 잠드는 시간을 몇 분 줄였다. 불면 치료는 아니다.
  오래가는 불면은 CBT-I로 안내한다(007).
- 6시간 수면이 2주 이어지면 수행 저하는 계속 쌓이는데 본인은 조금 졸린 정도로만 느꼈다. "괜찮다"는 느낌보다
  수면 기록을 본다.
- 새벽 5~6시 운동하는 사람을 직접 연구한 자료는 없다. 시각은 모두 옮겨 쓴 것이다.
